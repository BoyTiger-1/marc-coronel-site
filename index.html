Former amateur boxer. Kidney transplant recipient. The one they call when a story has to land.

The chapters

Eight rooms he had to walk into

Scroll — or jump to a chapter below

  1. 01 Los Angeles · before 2011

    The Boxer

    Three hours in the gym, six days a week, with Pacquiao and Mayweather tape running behind him. He was chasing a pro card.

    What he kept instead was the training itself — the habit of showing up to something that hurts, on a schedule, whether or not it is working yet.

    Three-hour sessions, daily. Source: UCLA Health

  2. 02 2011 · age 26

    The Diagnosis

    A routine physical for an individual insurance policy came back clean. Two days later the clinic called him back in, and a biopsy put a name to it: focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Scarring in the filters of his kidneys, with no genetic cause anyone could find.

    No cure, no timeline. Just a number to watch, and a body that still looked like an athlete’s.

    FSGS is the most frequent primary glomerular disease to progress to kidney failure. Source: peer-reviewed epidemiology

  3. 03 Coverage runs out

    The Fall

    Once he aged off his father’s military coverage, the disease stopped being paperwork and became a bill. For years he held the line on ten to fifteen pills a day and looked completely healthy doing it.

    Then he went down on the bathroom floor, and woke up in an emergency room being told his kidneys were finished.

    10–15 medications a day, for years, with no visible symptoms. Source: UCLA Health

  4. 04 18 months in the chair

    The Grind

    Three sessions a week while a machine did what his body would not. The grief was harder than the needles.

    So he ran his head the way he used to run camp: books on adversity, speeches on repeat, and a breathing practice he still uses in the wings before a keynote.

  5. 05 2019 · four states

    The Search

    He listed himself in California, Arizona, Maryland and New York to widen the odds. Then his sister posted the story, FOX 11 ran it, and thirty-two people volunteered a kidney.

    One by one, every single one of them was ruled out.

  6. 06 December 10, 2019 · UCLA

    The Transplant

    Shawna Robinson, a friend of his sister’s, saw the story and offered. Anemia disqualified her; iron infusions brought her back. Four hours of surgery, and Marc was walking the hall before the day was out.

    The match came from the story being public. That is the entire thesis of the work he does now.

    Discharged in three days, no complications. Source: American Kidney Fund

  7. 07 2020 onward

    The Advocate

    He took the playbook back to the people still waiting. Lifestyle Athletics programs inside prisons, schools and companies. Kidney Walks. Youth co-chair on a Panorama City council seat. Lead ambassador for UCLA’s CORE Kidney Program.

    Then he stopped asking institutions for attention and started asking them for law.

    The City of Los Angeles and the State of California now formally recognize FSGS awareness — the Senate resolution passed 34–0. Source: California State Senate, SCR 87

  8. 08 Today

    The Consultant & Speaker

    TEDx, on The Courage to Ask: How a Simple Story Can Save a Life. Capitol Hill, on living-donor protections and Medicaid. American Kidney Fund Ambassador and Kidney Health Coach. Host of The Kidney Fighter Show and the Nephluence Project.

    And consulting work with health systems and health-tech teams on getting digital tools into patients’ hands. Same fight, bigger room.

Survival → purpose

The transplant wasn’t the ending.
It was the proof.

A stranger gave up a kidney because a story reached her. Everything Marc builds now runs on that one mechanism: make the human reality legible, and people move — donors, clinicians, product teams, legislators.

The years since have been spent turning that accident into a method, and handing it to the people who are still in the chair, still waiting on a call, still being talked about instead of talked to.
